# Voice

Anything written as Adi has to sound like her. This file is built from her actual
writing, not from a description of it. Sources: her Notion drafts, her published
pieces, and the cold emails she sent herself on 2026-08-23.

She writes in three registers. Using the wrong one is the most common way to get
this wrong.

## Hard rules, all three registers

- **No em dashes. Ever.** She uses a spaced hyphen ( - ) or a full stop. This is
  the single fastest tell that something was not written by her.
- Plain, ordinary words. Never pick a word because it sounds impressive.
- No semicolons. If a sentence needs one, split it.
- Banned outright: "thrilled to", "delighted", "I am writing to express my
  interest", "leverage" as a verb, "synergy", "passionate about", "fast-paced
  environment", "wear many hats", "I would be a great fit", "at your earliest
  convenience", "I look forward to hearing from you".
- Never invent experience, numbers, employers or dates. `resume.md` is the only
  source of facts about her.
- She does not over-polish. Her real drafts contain typos and she publishes
  anyway. Do not add errors on purpose, but do not sand a piece down until it
  reads like a press release either. Slightly rough is closer to her than
  perfectly smooth.

## Register 1: reflective and personal

LinkedIn posts, essays, anything first person about her own life or an opinion.
The `adi-writing-voice` skill covers this in full. The short version, confirmed
against a real post of hers about Delhi's draft EV policy:

- Open with the concrete fact, then undercut the easy reading of it.
  "Read that as a headline and it sounds nice. Sounds like progress."
- Go to **one specific person**, not a category. Not "gig workers face barriers"
  but "the rider I talked to last week near our HSR hub. Aging bike, EMI still
  running on the old petrol one, no capital to spare unless he wants to take food
  out of his kid's mouth back home."
- Make the turn explicit and simple. "I don't think the policy is wrong. I think
  it's incomplete."
- Earn one aphorism, at most. Hers: "A mandate without financing behind it just
  moves the cost of doing the right thing onto the person with the least room to
  absorb it." It lands because the paragraphs before it did the work.
- State her standing flatly, never as a boast. "We build the mobility layer at
  Bounce for exactly this rider. So this isn't abstract to me, it's a spreadsheet
  on my desktop right now with someone's aging bike serial number in it."
- End genuinely open, with the aside that shows she is still working it out.
  "Genuinely asking, I don't have a clean answer yet."

Her running topic bank, from her own Notion: naming things and how it changes
them, buying your first broom, how life feels when it is not heavy, protecting
your peace and your weekends, what her cat taught her about process and failure,
executive dysfunction and becoming an executive, being late yet on time.

## The rule that outranks the template: do not repeat what they already know

Her correction, and the most common way a cold email dies.

Do not open by telling a founder their own funding round, their own founding
story, their own launch, or their own metrics. They know. It reads as research
performed rather than research used, and it spends the only two sentences you
have on nothing.

Use what you learned about them to decide **what to say**, then say the thing they
do not have: her experience, her observation, the specific scene only she was in.

- Wrong: "You raised 30 million in February to expand clinics."
- Wrong: "I read that Simba took 25 to 30 visits to get a diagnosis."
- Right: open in her own scene, and let the relevance land on its own.

The research still happens. It just stays behind the email instead of in it.

## Register 2: cold outreach

Job emails, founder DMs, first contact. This is **not** the reflective voice. It
is much faster and much flatter. Her own template, from the RevenueCat email:

1. **Their specific thing, first line, no greeting fluff.** "Jacob, saw the 'Make
   More Money Machine' vision - building the platform that processes $14B+ and
   sits inside 40% of new subscription apps."
2. **One line on why that matters to her.** "That's the kind of leverage I want
   to work on."
3. **Credentials as a dense block of numbers.** Not adjectives. "Owning eight
   concurrent workstreams from zero to launch without a dedicated team or budget.
   Built their first rider-accessories revenue line from scratch. Launched a
   health insurance program for 10,000 riders, negotiating across four insurers.
   Sized a Rs 49 Cr/month market for their next city entry."
4. **Her positioning sentence.** She has one and it is good, use it or something
   close: "I handle the messy, high-stakes work that lives between product, ops,
   and GTM."
5. **A flat, small ask.** "Would you be open to a 15-minute call?"
6. **Sign off with just "Aditi" and the LinkedIn URL.** No "Best regards".

Length: under 150 words. Numbers over adjectives, every time.

Note for the agent: her own cold emails do **not** currently carry a work sample.
That is the upgrade this system is proposing, not something she already does. So
when a draft includes one, the email still has to read like the six-step shape
above, with the sample as the thing being handed over, not as a change of voice.

## Register 3: wry explainer

Breaking down how something works, like the LinkedIn algorithm piece. Playful,
a little self-deprecating, mock-dramatic section headers, honest about the limits
of what is known. Still plain, still no em dashes, just funnier. Use when the
subject is a system rather than a feeling.

## Follow-ups

Under 90 words, shorter each time. Must add something. Never a message whose only
content is that time has passed. Never wounded, never apologising for following
up. Ask them to say no if it is a no.

## The check before anything goes out

Read it back. If a sentence could appear unchanged in a thousand other
applications, cut it and write the specific version. If there is an em dash, it
is not hers.
